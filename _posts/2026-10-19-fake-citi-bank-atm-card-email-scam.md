On Saturday, October 17, a message arrived with the subject line "CITI BANK LTD." Gmail had already dropped it into the spam folder, which is exactly where it belongs. The sender claims to be "Charles P. Retting" from Citi Bank. The body says an ATM card holding **$90 million** has arrived at a branch "a few hours ago," that delivery is "scheduled for today," and that you must reply immediately with your full name, country of origin, delivery address and occupation.

If you read that and rolled your eyes, good. But scams like this one survive for a reason: they are cheap to send, they cost the scammer almost nothing, and they only need a tiny number of people to reply. If you run a business, a startup or a local shop, you are a more valuable target than the average inbox owner, because scammers assume you have a bank account, a phone number that answers and a habit of replying to messages quickly.

This article breaks down the email line by line, explains what it really is, and gives you a practical checklist to protect yourself and your team.

## What the Fake Citi Bank Email Actually Says

Here is the message, summarized in plain language:

1. A person named "Charles P. Retting" says he writes from "CITI BANK LTD."
2. Your ATM card, "associated with the amount of $90 million," has arrived at the branch.
3. Delivery is "scheduled for today."
4. To proceed, you must send your full name, country of origin, current delivery address and occupation.
5. They "await your prompt response."

Notice what is missing. There is no account number, no reference number, no customer ID, no named branch, no phone number and no link to a real Citi page. The email never explains why you would be owed $90 million in the first place. It simply creates excitement and urgency, then asks for personal details.

## 9 Red Flags That Prove This Is a Fake Bank Email

### 1. The sender uses a free Gmail address

Large banks send customer messages from their own domains. This message came from a free Gmail account (we are not publishing the full address here, since free accounts are sometimes hijacked). A real banker would not announce a $90 million card delivery from a free mailbox.

### 2. You are in "bcc"

The header shows the message was sent "to bcc: me." That means the sender blasted the same email to a hidden list of people. A bank that owed you money would address you directly, by name.

### 3. The amount is absurd

$90 million on an ATM card is not a thing. Cards have daily withdrawal limits, and banks do not courier giant sums to strangers on plastic cards.

### 4. You have no account with them, and no reason to be owed money

You did not win anything, inherit anything or open an account. Unexpected money is the oldest bait in the book.

### 5. The bank name looks off

"CITI BANK LTD" reads like a generic label, not a precise legal entity. Real banks use specific registered names on official communication. Scammers lean on famous brand names and add "Ltd" to look formal.

### 6. The email asks for personal details instead of verifying yours

A real bank already holds your identity data and verifies you through secure channels. This message asks you to hand over your name, country, address and occupation by plain email.

### 7. The "occupation" question is a clue

Why would a card courier need your job title? Because scammers use it later to tailor the story. A business owner might get a "tax clearance" fee. A freelancer might get a "licence" fee. The profession helps them pick the next lie.

### 8. Artificial urgency

"Delivered today" and "contact us immediately" are pressure tactics. Scammers want you to reply before you think.

### 9. Gmail already flagged it

The spam banner says the message is similar to others identified as spam. Gmail's filters have seen this template many times, which tells you how widely it is being circulated.

## What Kind of Scam Is This?

This is a classic **advance fee scam**, a close relative of the well known "419" fraud, named after the section of the Nigerian criminal code it violates, as the FBI [explains in its guide to common frauds](https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/common-frauds-and-scams/business-and-investment-fraud){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. The pattern is always the same:

- **Bait:** A huge sum of money, a prize, an inheritance or a card waiting for you.
- **Hook:** You reply with personal details.
- **Fee:** Someone says you must pay a "delivery," "security keeping," "clearance" or "tax" charge before the money is released.
- **Repeat:** Every payment is followed by a new problem and a new fee, until you stop paying.

The money never existed. The only real money in the story is whatever you send.

Older versions of this exact scam used fake FBI, IMF and "ATM card center" letters. The branding changes, now it is Citi, tomorrow it will be another famous bank, but the script barely moves.

## The Numbers Behind Bank Impersonation Scams

This is not a small-time problem, so here are a few figures from official sources.

- The U.S. Federal Trade Commission reported that people lost **$3.5 billion** to imposter scams in 2025, nearly triple the 2020 figure. Business impersonators, with bank impersonators near the top, accounted for close to $1 billion of that. ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"})
- Total reported fraud losses to the FTC hit about **$16 billion** in 2025, the highest on record and roughly 25 percent higher than the year before.
- The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded **191,561 phishing and spoofing complaints** with about **$215.8 million** in reported losses in 2025, and roughly **7,762 advance fee complaints**, according to the [2025 IC3 annual report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.
- The Anti-Phishing Working Group counted **971,181 phishing attacks** worldwide in the first quarter of 2026, up 13.8 percent from the previous quarter. ([APWG Q1 2026 trends report](https://docs.apwg.org/reports/apwg_trends_report_q1_2026.pdf){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"})

One more point worth remembering: most of these figures only count people who reported the crime. The real total is higher.

## Why Business Owners and Startups Should Care

You might think, "I would never reply to that." Fair. But consider how this plays out in a small company.

- **Shared inboxes.** The info@ or hello@ mailbox is often managed by an intern, a virtual assistant or a busy founder skimming 80 messages before lunch.
- **Public contact details.** Your email is on your website, your Google Business Profile and your directory listings. Scammers scrape all of it.
- **Real money moves fast.** Small businesses handle invoices, vendor payments and card details daily. One careless reply can open the door to a more convincing follow-up scam, such as a fake invoice or a fake "bank verification" call.
- **Your brand can be impersonated too.** The same tricks used against Citi can be used against you. Scammers can send fake emails that claim to come from your shop, your agency or your startup. We covered a related tactic in our guide to the [fake Cloudflare CAPTCHA scam](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/29/fake-cloudflare-captcha-scam-business-guide.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, where attackers borrow a trusted brand name the same way.

## What Happens If You Reply? The Typical Playbook

Understanding the next steps helps you spot the scam even if you only see part of it.

| Stage | What the scammer does | What it looks like to you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Collect | Confirms your email is active and gathers name, address, occupation | A friendly "verification" reply |
| 2. Build trust | Sends fake documents, a fake tracking number or a fake ID card | Looks official at a glance |
| 3. First fee | Asks for a small "courier" or "security" payment | Feels cheap compared to $90 million |
| 4. Extra fees | Invents customs, tax, insurance or legal charges | "Just one last payment" |
| 5. Pressure or threats | Warns of legal trouble if you stop | Fear replaces logic |
| 6. Resale | Sells your details to other scammers | More scam emails and calls |

If you stop at stage 1, you have still confirmed your address to a criminal, which is worth something to them.

## Real Bank vs Scam Email: Quick Comparison

| What to check | Real bank message | This scam email |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sender address | Official bank domain | Free Gmail account |
| Greeting | Uses your name or account details | Generic, sent to hidden "bcc" list |
| Reference details | Account or case number | None |
| Request | Directs you to log in through the official app or website | Asks you to reply with personal details |
| Tone | Calm and specific | Urgent and vague |
| Offer | Normal banking business | $90 million card from nowhere |
| Contact route | Published branch or helpline numbers | Reply to the sender's email |

## What To Do Right Now

### If you only received the email

1. **Do not reply, click, download or call.** Even a reply saying "stop" confirms your address is active.
2. **Report it as phishing in Gmail.** Open the message, use the three dot menu and choose "Report phishing."
3. **Delete it** after reporting.
4. **Warn your team** if it reached a shared business inbox.

### If you replied or shared personal details

1. **Stop all contact** with the sender immediately.
2. **Do not send money.** If you already did, contact your bank or payment provider right away and ask about stopping or reversing the payment.
3. **Watch your accounts and credit.** Change passwords, turn on two-factor authentication and monitor for unusual activity, especially if you shared your address and occupation.
4. **Expect follow-up scams.** Be extra careful with unexpected calls, texts or invoices in the next few weeks.
5. **Report it** (see below).

### Where to report

- **United States:** [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and the FBI's [Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.
- **India:** the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, or call the national cyber crime helpline **1930** if money was lost.
- **The impersonated bank:** use the fraud reporting page on the bank's official website, which you reach by typing the address yourself, not by clicking anything in the email.

## Practical Tips To Protect Your Business From Fake Bank Emails

1. **Verify through a second channel.** If any message claims to be from a bank, close it and contact the bank using the number printed on your card or on its official website.
2. **Check the sender domain, not just the display name.** Anyone can type "Citi Bank" as a display name.
3. **Treat unexpected money as a warning sign.** Legitimate windfalls do not arrive by cold email.
4. **Never share identity details by email.** Banks do not ask for your address and occupation in a reply.
5. **Set a rule for shared inboxes.** Staff should forward suspicious messages to one person, not reply.
6. **Turn on two-factor authentication** for your email, banking and domain registrar accounts.
7. **Train your team for 15 minutes a quarter.** Show them real examples like this one. People remember a screenshot better than a policy document.
8. **Keep your spam filter on.** In this case it did its job.
9. **Use a separate email for public listings.** Keep your main banking and vendor email private.
10. **Document and report.** A short internal log of scam attempts helps you notice patterns.

## Protect Your Own Brand From Impersonation

Scammers do not only pretend to be big banks. Small businesses get copied too, and customers who get a fake message "from you" may blame you. A few steps help a lot:

- Publish a clear **Contact page** with your real email domain, phone number and address, and [increase your website trust score](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/15/how-to-increase-website-trust-score.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} with visible, verifiable business details.
- Add a short **"How we contact you"** note, for example: "We will never ask for payment details by email."
- Keep your **Google Business Profile**, directory listings and social profiles consistent, so customers can tell what is genuine.
- Use **[schema markup](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/26/schema-markup-generator-guide-why-it-matters.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}** and consistent business data so search engines and AI assistants display accurate details about you.
- Buy and renew the **[right domain names](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/29/buying-domain-name-for-business-guide.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}**, including common misspellings, and lock down your registrar account.
- Run a **[regular website audit](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/03/monthly-website-audit-checklist.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}** to catch outdated contact details, broken pages and trust gaps.
